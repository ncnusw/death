- **Story.** You doze off in the Week 8 lecture and wake up three years ago in the campus library, where your friend Zhe (阿哲) sits alone every day; his mother died suddenly 14 months earlier. Back then you walked past him. This time you go next door to the student counseling center, where 許老師 walks you through the whole lecture again, with Zhe's situation as the worked example. **Part 1:** resilience (71 / 24 / 5%), the three tiers of bereavement care, the more the need the more therapy helps, and the 38% myth. **Part 2:** PGD in DSM-5-TR, Criteria A–F, PGD vs depression vs PTSD, prevalence (1 in 10; 1 in 2 after unnatural deaths; the Taiwan caregiver study), risk factors, and the debate over calling grief a disorder. **Part 3:** the PG-13-R, the social worker's role (screen, don't diagnose; ask about suicide; refer), PGDT, the citalopram trial, and other evidence-based options. 11 quizzes on the English terms.
- **Discussion pause** after the counselor (password `YEARNING`, set in `tools/story.py`); the same password on the title screen jumps a new device straight to the second half.
- **The lower stacks.** Zhe has gone down to the basement, where the lights are out. Three floors of bookshelf mazes, lit only by your phone's flashlight (the torch effect from *Storm Over Clear Creek*). Eight drifting **shadows**, one for each Criterion C symptom (B1: avoiding reminders, emotional numbness; B2: intense loneliness, disbelief, life feels meaningless; B3: identity disruption, intense emotional pain, trouble re-engaging). The protagonist notes that grief itself is not the enemy; these are what keep it stuck. Each shadow's name appears when it comes into the light, and touching one sends you back to that floor's stairs. Each shadow crosses the route at a single junction, so you can always wait for it to pass. B1 and B2 are gentle; B3 is a bigger maze with a countdown (your phone battery), and the flashlight shrinks as it runs down. After two flat batteries, a hint and more time.
- **Finding Zhe.** A short conversation with choices: how to open (not "you should be over it by now"), listening instead of diagnosing, asking directly and gently about suicidal thoughts (he says no), describing the counseling center (free and confidential), and answering "does counseling mean I'm sick?". Then you walk him to the counselor, and wake up in class for the key takeaways. No hotline numbers are named; resources are kept generic.

## 2026-10-27
- Added **Bereavement Shift** (`games/bereavement-shift/`) for Week 8, *Grief counseling and prolonged grief disorder*. A top-down bereavement clinic for the projector: 1–2 players (8BitDo controllers or one shared keyboard: P1 WASD + F, P2 arrows + Enter), or one player on a tablet or phone (tap a person, then a room). Players are social workers who take each grieving person by the hand, screen them with the PG-13-R, and walk them to the care that fits: **watchful waiting** (resilient grief: leaflet, support, follow-up), **support group** (lonely or low, not PGD), **PGD therapy** (one couch, about 16 sessions), **family room** (high-conflict or low-communication families, about 10 sessions), or **referral** (mainly depression or trauma). Families move slowly with one player and quickly when both players hold hands. Chinese and English throughout; prolonged grief disorder is 延續性哀傷障礙症, as used by Taiwanese psychiatrists.
- A short care note opens the game: the cards are simplified for teaching and can't diagnose anyone, and if the game brings up a personal loss it's okay to step away; the 1925 line is listed.
- **Orientation** (about 5 minutes): resilience first; how to read a PG-13-R card and the DSM-5-TR rule (at least 12 months; yearning or preoccupation at 4+; at least 3 of the other 8 at 4+; daily life affected); three practice cards; a tour of the rooms with the evidence behind each; controls.
- **Levels**: Level 1, *Open clinic* (easy: cards shown, three rooms); Level 2, *Screen first* (moderate: cards hidden until screened at the desk, which needs a player standing there; "I'm fine" clients, depression- and trauma-led decoys, a family); Level 3, *The year turns* (difficult: Mrs. Tsai, who lost her only child, is too early for PGD at 8 months and returns for re-screening at 12 months; an anniversary dip that needs support, not treatment). A **Levels** menu, and a **Level code** for the challenge level (*Full house*), as in Palliative Shift. Clients who wait too long leave; a person in the wrong room comes back out with an explanation and waits to be moved. After each screening the card pops up large so the class can read it.
- Each level ends with a case log (what you chose, what fits, and the key point for every client). The end screen shows the percentage placed in the right room the first time on a full-screen green / yellow / red background, with screened-when-needed, wrong rooms and people who left, plus three discussion questions. Sources are on the About screen (Prigerson et al., 2021; DSM-5-TR; Bonanno, 2004; Currier et al., 2008; Shear et al., 2005; Kissane et al., 2016; Zhang et al., 2026). Locked with a class code. Pauses when the slide is hidden. Uses the site's own sounds.
- Added **Palliative Shift** (`games/palliative-shift/`) for Week 8, *Palliative care*. Students play the social worker on a palliative care ward, one player on their own phone, in landscape. Every screen is in Chinese and English together. A short notice opens the game every time: it is a simplified classroom review based on the Palliative Care Knowledge Test (PCKT), it is not medical advice, never change any medicine because of it, and social workers do not prescribe.
- **Orientation** (about 10 minutes): Nurse Hsu opens the six drawers of the ward's medicine cabinet one at a time (opioids such as morphine; pentazocine and buprenorphine; NSAIDs; steroids; sedatives; equipment and comfort care). For each drawer: tap to open it and read what it is for, flip the cards to see what families often ask, then answer a quick check. The last page shows three kinds of answer to a family: accurate and within the social work role, repeating a myth, or stepping outside the role. Can be skipped; the **Drawer Guide** reopens any drawer during play.
- **Three shifts**: day shift (one patient at a time), evening shift (two at once) and night shift (three at once, some in their last days); 15 patients in all. Each patient has three steps: at the bedside, flag the problem to the team; at the nursing station, watch which drawers the nurse opens, then pick them from memory (or "no drawer"); in the family room, choose how to answer a worried relative. Each step has a timer, and the patients you are not with keep waiting. Feedback after every step gives the reason, the nurse's explanation, and a better answer. One patient dies peacefully during the night shift, shown as a quiet moment with no score sound. A summary after each shift lists the points to remember.
- The end screen shows one very large percentage on a full-screen green (80%+), yellow (60–79%) or red (below 60%) background, with counts for needs flagged, drawers recalled, families answered accurately and within the role, and myths repeated and times the role was overstepped. Buttons for three discussion questions and the key points missed.
- Content: all 18 remaining PCKT items are taught in new wording; no item wording is used, as the PCKT is also given as a pre/post test. Answers follow the PCKT key throughout, including pentazocine and buprenorphine (items 4 and 6). Items 12 (anticholinergic drugs for bronchial secretions) and 14 (benzodiazepines for delirium) are left out, as the test's authors now recommend. Patients, families, and pill colours and shapes are fictional; no doses are shown. The citation is on the About screen.
- Locked with a class code, like **In Their Footsteps**. Touch, keyboard (arrows or 1–4, Enter, Esc = pause) and 8BitDo (D-pad, any face button, Start = pause). Pauses when the slide is hidden. Uses the site's own sounds: `wow.mp3` at the end of a shift, `win.mp3` at the end of the game.
- **Palliative Shift:** the big percentage on the end screen and after each shift is now accuracy only (correct steps out of all steps), so getting every step right shows 100% however long you take to read. Points still include the speed bonus and are shown smaller.
- **Palliative Shift:** added a fourth shift, **Challenge: full ward**. Five patients are on the ward at once (seven in all). Each has an urgency tag on the patient list: URGENT timers run almost twice as fast, STABLE ones about half as fast, so you have to decide who to see first; no patient is picked for you. At the station you don't see the nurse prepare anything. Guess the drawers from what you flagged at the bedside, then choose **Guess** (full points and speed bonus if right; if wrong, no points and the reminder never to pass a guess on to a family as fact) or **Check** with the nurse (always right, but it takes 5 seconds while the other patients keep waiting).
- **Palliative Shift:** a **Level code** button on the title screen jumps straight to the challenge shift, for testing. Only a hash of the code is stored in the page.
- **Palliative Shift:** rebalanced the medicine cabinet so the opioid drawer is no longer the answer for most patients (now 6 of 15, down from 9). Ms. Huang now comes in with poor appetite during chemo (steroids); Mrs. Kuo, whose pain is already controlled, gets no new medicine, and nothing may be added; Mr. Su's back pain is treated first with an NSAID. The opioid drawer in the orientation and Drawer Guide now notes that opioids are the most common medicine on a palliative ward, but each patient is assessed individually.
